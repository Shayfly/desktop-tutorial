# Portfolio

Covers: a creative person or studio showing finished work to win new commissions: graphic and brand designers, illustrators, lettering artists, architects, interior designers, digital agencies, animators. The work is the proof; the visitor is a possible client.
Does not cover: photographers (use `photographer.md`, which adds bookings, client galleries and prints); a writer's or thinker's own home on the web (use `personal-site.md`); a list of items for sale or loan (use `catalogue-collection.md`); a craftsman's renovation jobs (use `craftsman-renovation.md`).
Written 2026-11-22. References checked 2026-11-22. Template: [README.md](README.md).

## 1. Objects

A portfolio sells **trust in a way of working, shown through past results**. Everything follows from that: a project needs pictures that are really the owner's work, enough words to say what the problem was and what the owner did, and permission to show it. A visitor decides from the first three projects whether to keep looking.

### How objects relate

An owner has projects. A project has one discipline (sometimes more), optionally a sector, and many images. A project may name a client, but only with permission. Credits link a project to the people who made it with the owner. Featured projects appear on the home page; all projects appear on the work page.

## 2. Rules

1. Every published project has a title, a year, a discipline, a cover image and a one-line summary.
2. Every image in a project is work the owner made or led. Others' work in the same project is credited to them.
3. A client is named, and a client logo is shown, only with permission. Work under a confidentiality agreement is not published, or is published without the name and the identifying details, as the customer decides.
4. A project says what the owner did. "Brand identity" alone does not say whether the owner designed it or managed it.
5. Collaborators are credited when they did a visible part of the work (photography of the work, development, illustration).
6. A result or number in a case study is a fact the customer supplied and can back. No invented reach, sales or growth figures.
7. Awards, press and client quotes are shown only if real, with their source and year.
8. The owner chooses the order of projects. The default order is the owner's best first, not the newest first, unless the owner says otherwise (section 4).
9. A project removed at a client's request is hidden at once, including from the home page, the grid and any client list.
10. The way to start a project is reachable from every page without scrolling back to the top on a phone.
11. Every image has alt text. Text that matters (a lettering piece, a poster) is also written in the caption or alt text.
12. Video does not autoplay with sound. Motion can be paused, and the site respects a visitor's "reduce motion" setting.
13. The site works without a special scroll effect. Custom scrolling never stops a keyboard or screen-reader user from reaching content.
14. Large images are resized for the screen they are shown on. A phone does not download the full print file.
15. A photograph of a recognisable person in a project (a model, a client's staff) is published only with that person's consent.
16. If the owner is not taking new work, the site says so near the enquiry action, with when they will be.
17. A project page links to the next project, so the visitor never hits a dead end.

## 3. Mandatory facts

The product is not built until each of these has an answer from the customer. None may be guessed.

| # | Fact | The one question to ask | Why (the human reason) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Name as shown | "What name should be on the site: your own name or a studio name?" | Clients remember and recommend a name. |
| 2 | What you do | "In one sentence, what do you make, and for whom?" | A visitor decides in seconds whether you are the right kind of designer. |
| 3 | The projects | "Which projects do you want to show first? Pick the ones you would like more of." | The portfolio brings more of whatever it shows. |
| 4 | Pictures of each project | "Do you have pictures or files of the finished work for each one?" | Without images there is no portfolio. Stock images or mock-ups presented as real work are not allowed. |
| 5 | Permission per project | "For each project, may you show it, and may you name the client?" | Showing work under a confidentiality agreement can cost the client relationship or a lawsuit. |
| 6 | Your role per project | "What exactly did you do on each one, and who else worked on it?" | Clients hire you for what you did. Leaving out a partner's credit can end a working relationship. |
| 7 | How to start a project | "How should a new client reach you: email, a form, a call?" | The whole site leads to this one action. If it is wrong, the site produces nothing. |
| 8 | Where you work | "Where are you based, and do you work with clients elsewhere?" | Many clients want someone local, or need to know you work remotely. |
| 9 | Who is responsible | "What is the business name and number, for the legal pages?" | The privacy and terms pages must name who is responsible. |

Needed when they apply:

| # | Fact | The one question to ask | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | Availability | "Are you taking new work now? From when?" | A client who waits weeks for an answer goes elsewhere. |
| 11 | Clients, awards, press | "Are there clients, awards or articles you can show? Which may be named?" | Proof helps only when it is real and allowed. |
| 12 | Price signals | "Do you want to show prices or a minimum budget, or keep pricing for a conversation?" | It filters out enquiries you would refuse. Hiding it is allowed; inventing it is not. |
| 13 | Shop, classes, books | "Do you also sell prints or products, teach, or have books?" | These change the site from a portfolio to a portfolio with a shop. |
| 14 | Where enquiries go | "Which email should each new enquiry go to?" | An enquiry nobody sees is a lost commission. |
| 15 | Regulated title | "Are you a registered architect (or another protected profession)?" | Some titles may only be used by registered people (see Legal). |

## 5. Failure modes

| What went wrong | What the visitor sees | What it costs | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Huge images | A blank page for several seconds on a phone | The client leaves before seeing any work | Rule 14 |
| Video plays with sound, or heavy motion | Startled, or feels sick; cannot stop it | Leaves; may be an accessibility failure | Rule 12 |
| Custom scrolling breaks the keyboard | Cannot reach the projects or the contact | Excludes people; looks broken | Rule 13. Reference 3 publishes an article about its own scroll library, so the agency world knows the trade-off |
| No words on projects | Pretty pictures, no idea what the owner did | The client cannot judge fit; picks someone who explains | Rule 4; Skeleton B asks for the problem and the role |
| Client name shown without permission | Nothing, until the client sees it | Lost client; a breach of contract | Rule 3; mandatory fact 5 |
| Collaborator not credited | A partner finds their work under someone else's name | A public dispute; lost partner | Rules 2 and 5; mandatory fact 6 |
| Invented results | Numbers that the client can check and disprove | Credibility gone | Rule 6 |
| Contact hidden in the footer | Scrolls a long page looking for how to hire | Lost commission | Rule 10 |
| Old work leads | The first project is ten years old | Looks out of date; brings the wrong kind of work | Ask the customer for order (fact 3); prompt yearly |
| Broken embed | An empty box where a video or slideshow was | Looks neglected | Check embeds at publish and on a schedule; host video files where possible |
| Error text left on the page | A visitor or search engine reads "Something isn't loading properly" | Looks broken even when it is not | Reference 7's extracted text includes this sentence, probably a hidden fallback message; not confirmed in a browser. Never ship fallback text that shows by default |
| Lettering or poster text only in the image | Screen-reader users and search engines get nothing | Excludes people; the work is not found | Rule 11 |
| Shop takes over | The first screen sells merchandise, the work is one tile away | Clients looking to commission work have to dig | Reference 6 (a shop announcement leads) shows this; use Skeleton A unless most income comes from the shop |
| Enquiry form sends nowhere | "Sent" - and nothing ever arrives | A commission lost silently | Mandatory fact 14; test the form at setup |
| Not available, still says "start a project" | Writes, waits, hears nothing | Bad word of mouth | Rule 16 |

## 7. Legal

A checklist of questions, not legal advice. The author is not a lawyer and did not read the statutes for this file. Every item needs checking by someone qualified in the owner's country before it is relied on.

### Applies almost everywhere

- **Who owns the work.** After a commission, copyright in the finished work may belong to the client by contract. The designer's right to show it in a portfolio is often a contract clause, not automatic. Not verified per jurisdiction; ask the customer what their contracts say.
- **Confidentiality.** Work under a non-disclosure agreement must not be shown, or must be shown as agreed.
- **Trademarks.** Client names and logos are trademarks. Showing them to describe one's own work is common, but permission is the safe path.
- **Moral rights.** In many jurisdictions authors have a right to be credited. Leaving out a collaborator, or presenting their work as one's own, can breach it. Not verified.
- **People in images.** A recognisable person in a project image (a model, staff, the public) may need to have consented. Not verified per jurisdiction.
- **Privacy notice.** Needed as soon as there is an enquiry form, a newsletter or analytics.
- **Marketing emails.** A newsletter needs prior agreement and an easy way to unsubscribe in most places.
- **Shop.** Selling prints or products brings consumer law (returns, delivery, prices with tax). Reference 5's shop states a no-returns policy except for damage; whether that is allowed depends on the buyer's country (EU buyers have a withdrawal right for distance sales, with exceptions). Not verified.

### Israel

- Accessibility: businesses serving the public must make websites accessible and publish an accessibility statement, under the Equal Rights for Persons with Disabilities Law and its regulations, with Israeli Standard 5568 as the reference. Exemptions exist for very small businesses by turnover; whether a one-person studio is exempt: not verified.
- Privacy: the Protection of Privacy Law applies to enquiry data. Amendment 13 came into force in August 2025. What it requires of a one-person studio: not verified.
- Copyright: the Copyright Law 2007 includes moral rights (the right to be named). How it applies to commissioned design: not verified.
- Architects: the title and practice are believed to be regulated through registration (Engineers and Architects Law). Not verified.
- Marketing messages: section 30A of the Communications Law requires prior consent for advertising by email or SMS. Details: not verified.

### European Union and United Kingdom

- GDPR (EU) and UK GDPR apply to enquiries and newsletters. Non-essential cookies need consent (ePrivacy rules in the EU, PECR in the UK).
- UK: "architect" is a protected title under the Architects Act 1997; only people on the register held by the Architects Registration Board may use it. Believed correct; not verified for this file.
- EU member states regulate architects separately; the profession is recognised across the EU under the Professional Qualifications Directive. Not verified.
- Germany and Austria are believed to require a legal notice (Impressum) on business websites. Not verified for current law.
- Accessibility: the European Accessibility Act (from June 2025) covers certain consumer services with a microenterprise exemption. A portfolio without a shop is likely out of scope; not verified.

### United States

- Copyright: work made by an independent contractor is not "work made for hire" unless a written agreement says so and it falls in listed categories; otherwise the designer keeps copyright unless assigned. Believed correct; not verified for this file.
- "Architect" is a title restricted by state licensing laws. Not verified per state.
- The Americans with Disabilities Act is applied by courts to business websites in many cases. Not verified.
- Email marketing: the CAN-SPAM Act sets rules for commercial email, including an unsubscribe method and a postal address. Not verified.

### What the product does with this

- The product may generate the **structure** of a privacy page, a terms page and an accessibility statement. It marks each as a draft that needs review.
- The product never states that the site "complies with" a named law.
- The product asks for permission per project (mandatory fact 5) and never publishes a project without an answer.
- The product never uses a protected professional title unless the customer confirms it (mandatory fact 15).

### Needs accounts

- The owner needs one as soon as anything is editable in the product.
- Team members need them only if several people edit.
- Visitors never need one. Private projects use a link or password, not an account.

### Needs payments

- Only when the owner sells prints, products, books, sessions or workshops (references 5 and 6 do).
- Default: no payments. A shop is added only when the customer asks for it.

### Using an existing profile

Many designers already have work on a portfolio platform or a social account. Ask before rebuilding everything: the site can link to those, but the main projects should live on the owner's own site, because a platform can change or close. The failure mode is a site whose project links all lead away to someone else's platform.

## 10. Skeletons

Structure only. No colours, logos, images or wording were taken from any reference.
